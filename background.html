Background and problem

What are people listening to, when they listen for a reason?

Music psychology can now describe why people listen — the functions, goals and situations that make a listening episode meaningful — considerably better than it can describe what they are listening to.

Put plainly: research can say a great deal about the purpose of a listening episode, and much less about the chords, keys and harmonic motion that were sounding during it. This page gives the evidence for that imbalance and explains why it persists. It then shows how the work collected on this site — a real-time harmonic analyser, its audit against music theory, a comparison with two independent analysers, and a working-memory account of the architecture — is designed to supply one missing piece: a time-resolved, tonality-sensitive description of musical structure that can be set beside what listeners do, feel and seek.

1 · What is already established

Several independent lines of research have already shown that the properties of music affect listeners. What remains open is which properties matter, at what moment, and for whom.

Musical cues

Mode, tempo, register, dynamics, articulation and timbre make distinguishable contributions to perceived emotion when they are manipulated systematically.

Eerola, Friberg & Bresin (2013)
Episodes, not stimuli

Emotional experience with music is organised into situated, functional episodes — five families described through six schemes, one of which is musical meaning in its structural, self and source senses.

Eerola, Kirts & Saarikallio (2025); Thompson, Bullot & Margulis (2023)
Goals in everyday life

In everyday listening, affect-regulation goals are reached in fewer than half of cases, and success is determined largely within contexts rather than by the listener alone.

Randall, Baltazar & Saarikallio (2023)
Strategy or music?

Both the regulation strategy and the music selected affect short-term stress outcomes, which leaves open what is specific to the music itself.

Baltazar et al. (2019)
Listener profiles

Listener characteristics relate to which relaxation-music clusters people choose, when those clusters are defined by high-level audio descriptors such as energy, acousticness and valence.

Tan et al. (2026)
Measurement

The constructs of the Episode Model have been operationalised and their content validity assessed with subject-matter experts, as the first stage of a dedicated instrument.

Kirts et al. (2026)

2 · The problem: two timescales that have not yet met

The Episode Model explains experience at the level of the episode. In their section on challenges and limitations, its authors describe the temporal nature of experience as a challenge for any account of emotion, and state the timescale their model addresses:

“The episode model attempts to capture a broad temporal context (spanning minutes) rather than explaining the triggering process within much shorter timescales (seconds).” Eerola, Kirts & Saarikallio (2025), Challenges and limitations

The authors also posit, drawing on a mapping of structural cues to emotions, that structural elements of music are likely to be important for Enjoyment–Distraction–Relaxation and Focus–Motivation episodes, and less important for the more culturally and individually determined Aesthetic–Interest–Awe, Connection–Belonging and Personal Emotional Processing episodes; they add that Aesthetic–Interest–Awe has been linked to harmonic, timbral and dynamic changes associated with chills. Testing that expectation requires structure to be measured. Musical events, meanwhile, happen in seconds: a cadence arrives, a chord turns minor, a key shifts. Between the two levels lies a question that neither answers on its own.

The question

Do time-resolved melodic and harmonic configurations within tonality explain person- and function-specific outcomes beyond high-level audio descriptors, tempo and rhythm, statistical expectancy, preference, familiarity and context?

The same authors anticipate where such work could come from:

“We also envisage that the episode model offers a fruitful set of structures and hypotheses for Music Information Retrieval research.” Eerola, Kirts & Saarikallio (2025), Future directions and applied perspectives

Automatic harmonic analysis is one of the places where that invitation can be taken up. A functional-harmonic label is a compact, theory-bearing description of what a listener hears at a given moment — far more specific than genre, and more interpretable than a summary acoustic descriptor.

3 · Why the gap persists

A coded review of 190 peer-reviewed studies on music and memory published between 2020 and 2025 (PubMed and JSTOR) measured how often research actually describes the musical structure it uses (Tuğral, 2026; coded dataset: doi:10.5281/zenodo.22258970).

Three causes recur. Disciplinary silos: large-scale health and psychophysiological research treats music as a generic stimulus — a familiar playlist, a preferred genre — while analytically rich music scholarship rarely reaches behavioural and physiological pipelines. Annotation that does not scale: functional harmonic analysis still depends largely on expert-annotated corpora, which limits it to small repertoires. Temporal misalignment: musical structure, behaviour and physiology unfold at different rates and are connected retrospectively, if at all, so there is no time-resolved harmonic representation to align them with.

4 · Analysis that unfolds with the music

Linking a musical event to what a listener does, feels or shows physiologically at that moment places a requirement on the analyser that score-level analysis does not: the label has to exist at the moment, built only from what has already sounded. Current learned analysers are not designed for this. AnalysisGNN and AugmentedNet are powerful score-level models. Both take a finished score as input and both use bidirectional context — AnalysisGNN across a graph of the score’s notes, AugmentedNet within fixed windows of about twenty bars of 4/4 — so the label at any point also depends on music that comes after it. That is appropriate for analysing a finished score, and it means the label cannot be available while the music is still unfolding.

musWM takes the opposite design. It is rule-based: a label is retrieved from a precomputed lookup and refined by rules that cite the textbook. It is causal: a label is formed from the notes held in a capacity-limited window and uses nothing that has not yet sounded. And it is explainable: every label carries the pitch-class set, bass, root, chord code and local key it was derived from, so the reason for a label can be read off the record and checked against the score. The result is a responsive analysis that unfolds with the music.

5 · What the work on this site contributes

Each component below answers one of these obstacles. The right-hand column states what each one does not yet establish.

ObstacleContributionBoundary
Structure is described thinly musWM describes music as it unfolds: for every onset, a local key and mode, bass, root, chord code and Roman numeral, derived in real time. Structure enters the record as harmonic function, not as genre or a summary descriptor. Summary Symbolic input; one broad tonal style so far (335 works of the When in Rome corpus).
Annotation does not scale Labels are produced deterministically from a precomputed lookup, without per-item expert annotation, and every intermediate step is recorded with the label, so each one can be checked against the score. Inspectable output is not the same as a validated analysis.
No analyser is an authority musWM, AnalysisGNN and AugmentedNet are aligned on the same onsets of the same scores. Where they agree and where they diverge is shown row by row, so disagreement becomes an object of study rather than hidden error. Roman-numeral analysis has no single correct answer; agreement is not correctness.
Labels must answer to theory Every label is graded against the one Kostka, Payne & Almén’s Tonal Harmony derives, with page references; the engine’s core tables are published at every chromatic step; rules added later were audited against the textbook, and every engine build is logged. Theory · Method The textbook grade tests naming given musWM’s reading of key and notes, not the reading itself.
Events and episodes run on different clocks The analyser is built as a capacity-limited working-memory window: it makes explicit which sounding events remain jointly available long enough to form a harmonic unit, which is the seconds-level quantity an episode account would need to link to. Theory → Cognitive Sciences The working-memory correspondence is a hypothesis, not a demonstrated psychological mechanism.
Learned analysers work after the fact musWM is rule-based and causal: each label is formed only from notes that have already sounded, so it exists during listening and can be set beside concurrent observations. Batch and real-time runs of the engine produce the same labels (Tuğral, 2026). Real-time capability concerns timing and traceability; it says nothing by itself about analytical accuracy.
Structure is not aligned with behaviour and physiology Each harmonic token is emitted as one record to which concurrent behavioural, cardiovascular and EEG observations are attached. In technical verification on the 335 scores, 46,770 tokens were each linked one-to-one to a complete record, and all 49,750 shared comparisons between batch and real-time analysis matched (Tuğral, 2026). Repeated with the engine version of 15 September 2026, the replay linked 47,969 tokens one-to-one to complete records, and all 56,992 shared comparisons matched. Streams in that check were synthetic: timing and linkage are verified, physiological validity is not.
Episodes need an event layer EpiMusWM records an episode hypothesis and its descriptive schemes alongside time-stamped musical events, participant-marked reactions and physiological observations, keeping each as a separate class of evidence (Tuğral, 2026, appendix). An instrument design; content validity and field use remain to be assessed.

6 · Questions this makes answerable

  1. Descriptor reliability. Where do independent harmonic analysers converge and diverge on identical scores, and how far does realistic disagreement propagate into models of affective outcome built on their output?
  2. Episode-specific relevance. Does the relevance of structural musical meaning vary across episodes as the Episode Model expects — larger for Enjoyment–Distraction–Relaxation and Focus–Motivation than for Connection–Belonging, Personal Emotional Processing and Aesthetic–Interest–Awe, bearing in mind the authors’ note that Aesthetic–Interest–Awe has been linked to structural changes associated with chills?
  3. Incremental value. Do time-resolved tonal and harmonic configurations explain goal attainment beyond high-level audio descriptors, listener characteristics, preference, familiarity and context?
  4. Reproducibility. Do person- and function-specific structural profiles reproduce across repeated, self-selected listening in everyday life?

Answers to these questions are a precondition for structure-informed personalisation: music selection or recommendation that can say, in musical rather than proprietary terms, what it chose and why, and that is judged by whether listeners reach their own goals.

7 · Distinctions kept throughout

episode ≠ musical event physiology ≠ emotion label Roman numeral ≠ ground truth association ≠ causal effect of structure technical verification ≠ human validation personalisation ≠ therapy high-level audio descriptors ≠ time-resolved tonal organisation
